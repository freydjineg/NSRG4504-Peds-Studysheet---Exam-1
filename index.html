Module 1: Pediatric Nursing Today

This module lays the foundation for the whole course. Children aren't small adults: their bodies, minds, and needs change quickly from infancy through adolescence, and every child is part of a family. You'll learn what pediatric nurses do and where they work, the two big care philosophies (atraumatic care and family-centered care), the family and social factors that shape a child's health, and current trends and issues.

🌱1.1 The Scope of Pediatric Nursing & Where Pediatric Nurses WorkSlides 2–7
Why pediatrics needs a developmental lens

Pediatric nurses care for children from birth through adolescence (around 19 years). The "patient" changes fast: what counts as a normal heart rate, how a child communicates, how they cope, what can hurt them, and how much medicine they need all shift as they grow. Young children often can't tell you what's wrong, so strong assessment and communication skills matter more than ever. Catching a developmental delay early leads to earlier intervention and better outcomes.

👶 The patient changes rapidly
Normal findings, communication, coping, safety risks, and medication needs all change with growth and development. You compare a child to their age group's expected findings, not to an adult.
👨‍👩‍👧 The child exists within a family
The nurse considers physical, emotional, developmental, cultural, social, and family needs, not only the diagnosis in front of them.
See the whole child, not only the illness
  • Promote health and prevent illness
  • Provide safe care during acute and chronic illness
  • Protect emotional and developmental wellbeing
  • Include the family as a partner
  • Prepare children for increasing autonomy (doing more for themselves as they age)
Practice settings across the continuum
SettingWhat the nurse does there
Primary care clinicRoutine health and developmental assessment, vaccination and screening, health promotion/prevention teaching, management of common illnesses, growth and milestone tracking.
School healthChronic-condition care plans (asthma, diabetes), medication administration, first aid and emergency response, vision/hearing screenings, making sure immunizations are up to date, managing outbreaks, coordinating with families and teachers.
Pediatric specialty daycareCares for children with complex medical needs while parents are at work: medications, equipment monitoring, condition-specific therapies, developmental support, a safe, nurturing environment.

Match the setting to its signature task: school nurse = keeps immunizations up to date and manages outbreaks; primary care = gives the vaccines and does developmental screening; ED = acute/life-threatening; specialty daycare = complex needs while parents work; specialty hospital = inpatient complex care.
🧸1.2 Atraumatic Care & Family-Centered CareSlides 8–12
Atraumatic care: minimize preventable distress

Atraumatic care means using interventions that minimize physical and psychological harm, so children don't come to fear health care workers or develop post-traumatic stress from medical care. The three principles: reduce stressors (including pain), limit separation from parents, and support the child's sense of control. Benefits: less stress, more sense of control, less fear and anxiety, and faster healing.

🫧 Distraction
Bubbles, music, videos, breathing, counting, conversation, guided imagery, all matched to age.
✋ Allow choices
Offer two safe options: which arm, sitting or lying, bandage color, who stays nearby. Never offer a choice that doesn't exist ("Do you want your shot?").
🎭 Therapeutic play
Use dolls, real equipment, rehearsal, and stories to explain procedures and help kids process experiences.
🛠️ Modify care
Manage pain (e.g., EMLA cream about 1 hr before an IV), use neutral language, keep the family present, do painful procedures in a treatment room (not the child's bed, their "safe space"), create a child-friendly environment.
Timing of preparation
  • Toddlers and preschoolers: explain right before the procedure (their sense of time is limited, and early warning just builds fear).
  • Older children: can be told days to weeks ahead.
  • For children school age and younger, encourage parents to stay, especially during painful procedures. Positioning on a parent's lap is often better than holding a child down on a table.
🚫 Not atraumatic
Restraining a child as the first choice, telling a child an injection "won't hurt" (dishonest, so trust is lost), sending parents out of the room, or planning care with the parents but leaving the child out.
Family-centered care: the family is the constant

Families bring expertise on the child's baseline, routines, communication, coping, preferences, culture, and response to illness. The health care team brings clinical expertise. Partnership combines both in care planning, decision-making, education, evaluation, and the transition home.

Core principleWhat it looks like
Respect & dignityHonor the family's perspectives, choices, values, beliefs, and cultural background.
Information sharingGive timely, complete, unbiased, understandable information.
ParticipationSupport children and families in care and decisions at the level they choose.
CollaborationWork together in care delivery, education, policy, quality, facility design, and research.
Atraumatic care asks…
What distress can we prevent? How can we preserve control? What will help this child cope?
Family-centered care asks…
What does this family know? How do they want to participate? What values should guide care?

Together they lower a child's stress because the team protects comfort, preserves connection, and shares control. That builds trust, coping, healing, satisfaction, and safer transitions home.

🛡️1.3 Roles of the Pediatric Nurse: Advocacy, Prevention, Ethics & the TeamSlides 13–17
Advocacy
  • Uphold nursing standards and speak up for the child's unique needs.
  • Address barriers, inequity, and social determinants of health (SDOH).
  • Protect safety and rights.
  • Nurses are mandated reporters. Report suspected neglect, abuse, or trafficking. You don't need proof. You report and document objectively; you don't confront, blame, or investigate on your own.
Therapeutic relationships
  • Build trust with the child and family.
  • Listen and validate emotions.
  • Communicate honestly (no "this won't hurt").
  • Maintain boundaries (e.g., don't connect with families on social media).
  • Use the connection to improve outcomes: sit at eye level, allow time for questions, offer a comfort item.
Health promotion & injury prevention

Prevention begins before illness. Nurses screen (growth, development, vision, hearing, mental health, social risks), educate (nutrition, dental care, vaccines, sleep, exercise, medication safety), anticipate (give age-specific anticipatory guidance before new developmental risks appear, at every well-child visit), and protect (safe products, car seats, safe sleep, water safety, safe storage).

📊 Leading causes of death to know
  • Unintentional injury is the leading cause of death for children 1–19 years (motor vehicle crashes, drowning, poisoning, falls, suffocation, firearms).
  • In infants, accidental suffocation is the leading cause of injury death (not choking).
  • Unintentional injury is the 3rd leading cause of death in infants 1–12 months.
Support, counseling & collaboration

Use trauma-informed care to calm fears. Support parents by talking often, listening, validating emotions, giving anticipatory guidance, and empowering them to take part. Don't place blame. Include parents and guardians as collaborators so they feel capable at home.

The "invisible wound" from severe or repeated moral distress: guilt, shame, anger, disengagement, burnout. Debriefing after traumatic events lowers the risk. Build moral resilience (know your values, communicate, mindfulness, self-care).

🏡1.4 Family & Social Influences on Child HealthSlides 18–23

Health is shaped far beyond the bedside. Family relationships, housing, food, school, peers, culture, faith, technology, safety, discrimination, and money can protect health or create risk. Trauma and adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) (abuse, neglect, household dysfunction) have long-lasting effects.

Every family structure deserves respect

Ask: Who does the child consider family? Who provides daily care? Who has legal authority? Who should receive information?

Single parentAssess support, time, finances, stress, and caregiving demands.
Divorce / coparentingPromote consistent routines and trauma-informed support. Teach: create a parenting plan everyone agrees on, set clear boundaries, let kids share emotions and listen, resolve conflict in private, show unconditional love.
Blended familyExpect evolving roles; set realistic expectations, communicate openly, parent together, celebrate differences, encourage child-centered respect.
Adoption / foster careAssess attachment, trauma history, records, transitions, and team supports; use trauma-informed care.

Authori-TATIVE = "talk-ative": they explain and listen. Authori-TARIAN = "tyrant": rules without reasons.
Effective discipline
Set clear expectations, keep a positive relationship, use developmentally appropriate limits, use positive reinforcement, redirect, and help the child understand consequences. Avoid corporal (physical) punishment.
Culture, religion & economics
🌍 Culture
Practice cultural awareness → knowledge → skill → humility. Ask instead of assuming; adapt care without stereotyping; explain the plan clearly without medical jargon; ask about home remedies.
🕊️ Religion & spirituality
Beliefs may shape coping, diet, modesty, rituals, treatment decisions, and family roles. Support practices safely and without judgment.
💵 Economic resources
Income affects food, housing, transportation, meds, equipment, childcare, and access to care. Low socioeconomic status raises risk for depression, anxiety, substance use, and injury.
Cultural competence attributeMeaning
Awareness (first step)Self-reflection on your own values, biases, and assumptions.
KnowledgeLearning evidence-based information about different groups' health beliefs and disease patterns.
SkillsCollecting culturally relevant assessment data and giving culturally sensitive care.
HumilityDesire, empathy, compassion; a lifelong commitment to seeing dignity in others.
School, peers & technology
  • School: builds literacy, thinking, social and conflict-resolution skills, future opportunity, and access to health services. Health outcomes improve with each year of school completed.
  • Peers & activities: peers can influence behavior and mental health positively or negatively; safe group play and activity support development.
  • Technology: digital media can support connection and learning but may crowd out sleep, activity, relationships, and attention.
Creating a safe place for every child to speak
🏳️‍🌈 LGBTQIA+ adolescents
Higher risk of abuse, depression, and suicidal ideation. Build trust, avoid assumptions, assess support and safety, protect privacy within legal limits, use inclusive language, ask directly about mental health/abuse/suicidal thoughts if distress is noted, and connect youth to affirming resources.
🗣️ Gender identity
Invite open conversation. Use the child's stated name and pronouns, document them in the record, and hold the team accountable for using them. Listen to goals and concerns; respond with acceptance (this promotes beneficence).
📈1.5 Current Trends & Issues: Prevention, SDOH, Digital Life, Trafficking & Chronic DiseaseSlides 24–30
Social determinants of health (SDOH)

SDOH are the everyday conditions that shape a child's opportunity to be healthy: economic stability (food, housing, income), education (access, quality, literacy), health care access (coverage, primary care, quality), built environment (safety, transportation, housing), and social context (support, belonging, discrimination). The nurse's job: Ask (screen respectfully) → Connect (referrals, e.g., social worker, food pantries, federal food programs) → Advocate (reduce barriers and inequity). A family struggling financially is a reason to connect them to resources, not a reason to call child protective services.

Three levels of prevention
LevelGoalExamples
Primary (BEFORE)Prevent disease or injury from ever happeningImmunizations, car-seat teaching, safe home environment, healthy-eating education
Secondary (EARLY)Detect disease or risk earlyVision, hearing, developmental, blood pressure, lead, and depression screening; dental exams; Pap smear
Tertiary (AFTER)Limit complications and restore functionRehab after a burn, PT after injury, asthma education, A1C monitoring/diabetes complication prevention
🧠 Prevention in one line
Primary = Prevent it · Secondary = Screen for it · Tertiary = Treat the damage.

Herd immunity: when enough people are vaccinated, disease can't spread easily, protecting those who can't be vaccinated. Childhood vaccination rates are falling below herd-immunity levels (e.g., polio and varicella coverage are below Healthy People 2030 targets), raising outbreak risk. Nurses promote vaccines by building trusting relationships, staying current on safety data, educating parents on benefits, coordinating care, and helping uninsured families get low/no-cost vaccines.

Digital life: connection or risk
📱 Social media
Assess content, relationships, privacy, sleep, activity, mood, safety, and what screen time is crowding out. Help families make a realistic media plan and household rules. Night-time use shortens sleep. Posts leave a permanent digital footprint. The Surgeon General recommends that children under 14 avoid social media. AAP: no screens until 18–24 months; then max 1 hr/day until age 5.
😞 Bullying & cyberbullying
Screen for repeated harm, power imbalance, isolation, school avoidance, somatic symptoms (headaches, stomachaches), mood changes, self-harm risk, and safety. Prevention: help kids identify bullying, teach safe ways to stand up, keep communication open, encourage hobbies, role-model kindness.
  • Younger children: frequent device use can displace conversation, hands-on play, and the chance to build language and emotional-regulation skills.
  • School-age/adolescents: sleep disruption, less activity, distraction, cyberbullying, social comparison, body image, violent/sexual content, misinformation, online predators, sexting, privacy loss.
  • Conversation prompts (open questions): "What do you enjoy online?" "What makes you feel worse?" "Have you felt pressured, threatened, excluded, or unable to stop?"
🚩 Warning signs of problematic use
Irritability when devices are removed, inability to stop, declining grades, sleep loss, withdrawal from family activities, secrecy, and changes in mood or behavior.
Human trafficking: recognize without increasing danger
Possible indicators
Controlling companion who won't leave or lets the child speak, inconsistent history, fearfulness, avoiding eye contact, delayed care or untreated injuries, signs of abuse or neglect/malnourishment, unusual tattoos or branding, STIs, frequent running away, unstable housing.
Nursing response
Create privacy when safe, use trauma-informed communication, assess immediate danger, do NOT confront the companion or investigate yourself (it can escalate danger), document objectively, involve the social worker, and follow reporting protocols. National Human Trafficking Hotline: 1-888-373-7888.

Slide data: in 2024 the National Human Trafficking Hotline identified 11,999 cases with 21,865 victims; reported demographics included 2,666 minors (age wasn't known for every victim).

Chronic health burdens are rising
⚖️ Obesity
About 1 in 3 U.S. children is overweight or obese. Multifactorial: genetics, environment, food access, sleep, stress, activity, screen time, medications, social conditions. Effects: hypertension, type 2 diabetes, sleep apnea, fatty liver, joint problems, early puberty, poor self-esteem, bullying. Use respectful, family-based care.
🌬️ Asthma
Assess triggers, inhaler technique, adherence, access to meds, action plans, school readiness, and early signs of deterioration.
🩸 Diabetes
Prevention where possible, early recognition, glucose management, nutrition, coping, family education, and safe school participation.

In every chronic condition, think about school plans, medication access, mental health, stigma, and the gradual transition to self-management.

Pediatric research requires added protections
Parental permission
Parents/legal guardians generally give permission for a child to be in research, unless an approved exception or waiver applies.
Child assent
Assent = the child's affirmative agreement when capable. Silence or failure to object is NOT assent.
🤝 The pediatric nursing promise
See the child. Partner with the family. Protect the future. Care that is developmentally appropriate, atraumatic and family-centered, and equitable, ethical, and collaborative.

💬2.1 Communication & the Pediatric Health HistorySlides 3–7
Ground rules for every assessment
  • Introduce yourself to the child and the caregiver.
  • Observe before touching. Assessment starts the second you walk in.
  • Use a calm voice and age-appropriate, jargon-free language; get at the child's eye level; make eye contact.
  • Let the child stay with the caregiver (on a lap is fine).
  • Begin with the least invasive techniques; save the scary or uncomfortable parts (ears, mouth, genitals) for last.
  • Use praise and reassurance to build trust.

Components of a pediatric health history
Current health concern
Chief complaint and history of present illness; symptom onset, duration, severity.
Past medical history
Chronic illnesses, hospitalizations and surgeries, medications, immunizations, and allergies (plus birth history in young children).
Developmental history
Age-appropriate milestones → school performance → behavioral concerns.
Family & social history
Hereditary conditions, home environment, caregiver concerns, environmental exposures (lead, smoke).

Until adolescence, parents provide or confirm most of the subjective data (what the client reports). Objective data is what you measure and observe.

🧠 Pattern to remember
As children grow, heart rate and respiratory rate go DOWN and blood pressure goes UP. Infants can breathe irregularly; after 12 months, breathing should be regular with no accessory muscle use.
🌡️ Temperature
Normal 36.1–37.2°C (97–99°F); newborn 36.5–37.5°C. Fever ≥ 38°C (100.4°F). Use axillary, temporal, or non-contact first to reduce fear. Tympanic: over 6 months. Oral: over 4 years. Rectal: infant supine with legs toward chest, older child side-lying; avoid with diarrhea, immunocompromise, bleeding disorders, or rectal surgery.
💓 Pulse
Take when the child is calm; crying raises it. In infants use brachial or femoral for rapid assessment, and count the apical pulse for a full minute. Note rate, rhythm, force, equality.
🩺 Blood pressure
Routine starting at age 3. Cuff width ≈ 80% of the limb; a cuff that's too small reads falsely high, too large reads falsely low. Hypertension = above the 95th percentile on 3 separate visits (<13 yr) or ≥130/80 (13+). Watch for "white coat" elevation.
👂 Tympanic technique (ATI convention)
Under 3: pull the pinna down and back. 3 and older: pull up and back (like an adult). (Your textbook describes "gently pulling up" for children over 6 months; know the ATI rule for test questions.)

  • Observe work of breathing (retractions, nasal flaring, grunting, accessory muscles).

Cardiovascular
  • Heart rate and rhythm.

Abdomen
⚠️ Order matters: Inspect → Auscultate → Palpate → (Percuss)
Listen before you touch so palpation doesn't change bowel sounds; it also relaxes the child. Auscultate starting in the RLQ, moving clockwise. Inspect shape, symmetry, umbilicus; palpate for tenderness, masses, organ enlargement, bladder distention. Ask about appetite, elimination, nausea/vomiting.
Genitalia & perineum
  • Purpose: evaluate growth, find congenital anomalies (e.g., hypospadias = urethral opening below the tip of the penis) or infection, assess urinary/reproductive health.
  • Inspect for symmetry, color, swelling, lesions, rash, discharge; assess perineal skin integrity and hygiene; palpate the scrotum/inguinal area for hernias (supine).
  • Explain in age-appropriate language, do it near the end of the exam, ensure privacy, involve the caregiver as appropriate. Adolescents are offered privacy and told about confidentiality within legal limits.
Musculoskeletal (observe)
  • Gait, posture, range of motion, muscle tone, symmetry, bilateral strength.
  • Spine: look for curvature (scoliosis) while standing and bending to touch toes.
  • Pulls to stand ~12 months; runs and climbs stairs ~2 years. An 18-month-old who can't pull to stand needs follow-up.
Neurologic (observe)
  • Speech, coordination, balance, behavior, alertness.
  • Infants: primitive reflexes and tone.
  • Most of the neuro exam in young children is done by watching them play.
😣2.5 Pain Assessment & ManagementSlides 18–22

Pain is the most commonly reported symptom in primary care, and in children it is often missed, undervalued, and undertreated. Pain is subjective, so use the child's own report when possible plus an age- and cognitively-appropriate scale, and reassess throughout the visit.

Types of pain
Acute (nociceptive): short-term; starts in the peripheral nervous system. Chronic: lasts 3–6+ months. Neuropathic: dysfunction of the somatosensory nerves. Centralized: lowered pain threshold after prolonged pain.
Assessment includes
Client and family history, pain severity (a scale), pain history, and quality, location, onset, duration, what makes it better or worse, and how it affects function.

📈2.6 Infant Growth, Reflexes & Motor DevelopmentSlides 5–7

Infancy is the period of the most rapid growth in life: physically, emotionally, and cognitively.

⚖️ Weight
Doubles birth weight by 4–6 months; triples by 12 months. Gain about 30 g (1 oz)/day in the first 3 months, slowing after 6 months.
📏 Length
Increases about 50% during the first year.
🧠 Head
Head circumference grows fast (brain growth). Growth should follow a consistent percentile. A head that lags far behind weight/length percentiles is a red flag.

Other maturation: rapid neurologic development, immune system maturing, better GI function, stronger muscles and bones. Growth is fastest in the first 6 months, then gradually slows.

🫶2.7 Infant Psychosocial, Cognitive & Language Development, Play & Red FlagsSlides 8–10
Erikson: Trust vs. Mistrust
Consistent, responsive caregiving (feeding when hungry, comforting when crying) builds trust and secure attachment. Unmet needs can lead to mistrust.
Piaget: Sensorimotor
Learns through senses and movement (mouthing, grabbing, dropping). Object permanence develops between 4 and 8 months (knowing things still exist when out of sight). Cause and effect begins.
Social milestones
  • 2 months: social smile; calms when spoken to; looks at faces.
  • 4 months: smiles on own to get attention; chuckles.
  • 6 months: knows familiar people, likes the mirror, laughs.
  • 9 months: stranger anxiety (shy/clingy with strangers); reacts when parent leaves; several facial expressions; responds to name.
  • 12 months: pat-a-cake; points to ask for things (proto-imperative pointing).
Language
  • Crying is the first form of communication.
  • Cooing ("oooh," "aah"): slide 8 says ~1–2 months; textbook places full cooing at ~4 months. Think "early months."
  • Babbling by ~6 months; "mamama/bababa" by 9 months.
  • First meaningful words ~12 months ("mama/dada"), waves bye-bye, understands "no."
  • Boost language by talking, reading, and singing to the infant often.
Play

Play promotes cognitive, motor, social, and emotional development. Good choices: tummy time (supervised, awake), soft books, mirrors, rattles, and interactive games like peek-a-boo (teaches object permanence).

Parents, separation anxiety & well-being
  • Encourage responsive caregiving and bonding; reinforce normal expectations; discuss well-child visits and immunizations.
  • Separation anxiety begins ~6–8 months (lasts to about 3 years) and is a sign of healthy attachment. Tell parents it's expected. Suggest leaving the infant with a trusted caregiver in a familiar place and not sneaking away.
  • Screen caregivers for postpartum depression (any new parent, birth or adoptive, is vulnerable) and stress; encourage self-care and support systems.
🚩 Notify the provider if an infant shows…
  • Persistent primitive reflexes
  • Loss of previously acquired skills (regression)
  • Poor muscle tone or asymmetrical movement
  • No social smile by ~2 months
  • No babbling by ~6 months
  • Can't sit independently by ~9 months
  • No attempts to crawl, pull to stand, or communicate by 12 months
  • Head lag after ~4–5 months
🛏️2.8 Infant Health Promotion: Nutrition, Safe Sleep, Safety & Common ConcernsSlides 11–14
Routine care & the nurse's role

Well-child exams throughout infancy, developmental surveillance and screening, recommended immunizations, and growth charts. The nurse assesses growth and development, teaches with anticipatory guidance, reinforces injury prevention, and gives family-centered care. Healthy environments need nurturing, stability, and safety.

Breastfeeding
  • Exclusive breastfeeding for about the first 6 months; continue with complementary foods through at least 12 months.
  • Gives optimal nutrition and passive immunity; lowers ear infections, respiratory infections, asthma, and SUID.
  • Feed on demand, about every 2–3 hr (8–12 times/day).
  • Do not breastfeed with HIV or active substance use.
  • Be nonjudgmental: "Both breastfeeding and formula will give your baby the nutrition they need."
Formula
  • Iron-fortified formula when breastfeeding isn't possible or chosen.
  • Use prepared formula within 2 hr (refrigerate up to 24 hr); discard leftovers in the bottle.
  • Never prop the bottle or put baby to bed with one (choking, ear infections, tooth decay).
Introducing solid foods
  • Around 6 months, when ready: good head control, sits with support, tongue-thrust reflex gone.
  • Start with iron-fortified cereal mixed with breast milk/formula, then vegetables, fruit, meat.
  • One new food every 3–5 days (to spot allergies).
  • Allergenic foods (egg, peanut) can be introduced before 1 year; early introduction lowers allergy risk.
  • No honey before 12 months (risk of infant botulism). No cow's milk or juice before 12 months.
Safe sleep (prevents SUID)
😴 The ABCs of safe sleep: Alone, on their Back, in a Crib
  • Always on the back for every sleep, including naps.
  • Firm, flat mattress that fits snugly.
  • Empty crib: no blankets, pillows, toys, or bumper pads.
  • Room-share without bed-sharing for at least 6 months.
  • Move to the crib if the baby falls asleep in a swing, car seat, or carrier.
  • Pacifier at sleep, avoid overheating, breastfeed, no smoke exposure.

Sleep amounts: 0–3 months about 16–17 hr/day; 6–12 months about 12–16 hr including naps (textbook 13–14). A regular schedule develops around 4 months. Let babies try to self-soothe back to sleep.

Activity & injury prevention
Activity
Daily supervised tummy time while awake (move to the crib on their back if they fall asleep), age-appropriate exploration, and limit restrictive equipment (swings, bouncers, car seats outside the car).
Injury prevention
Rear-facing car seat in the back seat; chest clip at armpit level; harness snug, no bulky coats/blankets under straps; get a bigger seat when the head is within ~1 inch of the top. Never leave the infant alone on high surfaces (falls). Keep small objects away (choking). Water safety and constant supervision. Childproof outlets and cabinets.

At 6 months, new rolling ability raises fall and suffocation risk; new grasping/mouthing raises choking risk.

Common infant health concerns
ConcernKey points
SUID (sudden unexpected infant death)Umbrella term for sudden death <1 yr, including SIDS and accidental suffocation. Risk factors: prone/side sleeping, soft bedding, bed-sharing, couch sleeping, smoke exposure, overheating, prematurity, young maternal age.
Feeding difficulties / bottle aversionMore common in preemies (NICU oral trauma) and GERD. Make feeding positive; refer to speech/OT if it persists.
Failure to thrive (growth faltering)Not enough calories, too much energy use, or poor absorption. Rule out medical causes (cardiac, cystic fibrosis, malabsorption) and social ones (neglect, formula mixing errors). Ask about intake, formula prep, wet diapers and stools.
Food allergiesCow's milk is the most common; then egg, peanut, soy, wheat. Can cause GI upset, eczema, asthma.
Vitamin DExclusively breastfed infants need vitamin D drops starting shortly after birth (use the provided dropper).
IronIron stores last until about 6 months. Preemies and exclusively breastfed babies are at risk; AAP suggests supplemental iron from 4 months until iron-rich foods are added.
Sleep disturbancesUse a calming bedtime routine (bath, book, bed).
Anticipatory guidance topics
Expected milestones, nutrition and feeding progression, injury prevention, safe sleep, recognizing illness, and the importance of routine well-child visits.

Seeks independence; enjoys simple choices; needs consistent routines and limits; positive reinforcement builds confidence. Give choices that can't be answered "no": "Red shirt or orange shirt?" not "Do you want to get dressed?"
Play

Play builds thinking, language, motor skills, creativity, emotional regulation, and social skills. Solitary play (early toddlerhood) → parallel play (plays beside other children with little interaction; the typical toddler stage) → beginning peer play at 2–3. Recommend safe, age-appropriate toys (push-pull toys, blocks, crayons, simple puzzles), active unstructured play, and limited screen time. Violent or sexualized play that repeats can be a sign of trauma.

Nursing considerations
Observe symmetry, coordination, and balance; encourage active play; reinforce age-appropriate expectations with parents; involve the parent during assessment (toddlers still have separation and stranger anxiety); let them play with the stethoscope first.

Toilet training

Usually ready between 2 and 4 years; don't try before 18–24 months. By about 2, the spinal cord is myelinated enough for the child to sense a full bladder/bowel.

Signs of readiness
  • Stays dry for at least 2 hours
  • Recognizes the urge to urinate or defecate
  • Follows simple directions
  • Can pull clothing up and down
  • Shows interest in the toilet
  • Communicates the need to go
Teaching
  • Positive, child-led approach; potty chair where feet touch the floor
  • Consistent toileting routine at regular intervals
  • Praise attempts; don't focus on accidents
  • Avoid punishment or criticism (it delays progress)
  • Expect setbacks with illness/stress; avoid starting during big changes
  • If the child resists, stop and retry in 2–3 months
🦺2.11 Toddler Health Promotion, Injury Prevention & Health ConcernsSlides 22–23
Health promotion
  • Nutrition: about 1,000–1,400 kcal/day in 4–6 small meals/snacks; balanced diet rich in iron, calcium, fruits, veggies, whole grains. Whole milk until age 2; limit milk to 16–24 oz/day. Water instead of juice/soda. Picky eating and food jags are normal: keep offering, don't force.
  • Choking prevention: quarter grapes, cut hot dogs lengthwise, smash blueberries; avoid popcorn, nuts, hard candy, raw carrots.
  • Sleep: 11–14 hr/day including naps; consistent bedtime routine; screens off 1 hr before bed.
  • Activity: at least 180 min/day of active play.
  • Dental: parents brush twice daily with fluoride toothpaste; first dentist visit by the first tooth or 12 months, then every 6 months; fluoride per provider.
  • Stay on the immunization schedule.
Injury prevention
  • Rear-facing car seat until at least age 2 and ideally until the seat's max height/weight; middle of the back seat; chest clip at armpit level; no puffy coats under straps; replace seats after any crash or expiration.
  • Drowning is a leading injury death at ages 1–4: fence pools with self-closing, self-latching gates, use pool alarms, never leave a child in the tub, toilet locks, empty buckets.
  • Burns: water heater ≤ 120°F (49°C); pot handles turned in; keep kids 3 ft from heat sources.
  • Poisoning: lock up meds and chemicals; post Poison Control (1-800-222-1222); don't induce vomiting unless told to.
  • Falls: gates on stairs, window guards, anchor furniture.
  • Close supervision, especially near water.

ConcernKey points
Lead poisoningNo safe level. Sources: lead paint in homes built before 1978, soil, old pipes, imported goods, parents' jobs/hobbies (construction, shooting ranges). Unit 2 – Mod 4 – Preschoolers: 3 to 6 Years

Slides: Lifespan Pt 1 (24–29) · Textbook: Unit 2 Mod 4
🦄2.12 Preschool Growth, Thinking, Language & PlaySlides 25–26
Growth & motor
Slower, steady growth, mostly in the long bones: about 4–5 lb and 2–3 in per year. Gross motor: balances on one foot → hops and skips; climbs stairs with alternating feet; rides a tricycle → bicycle; throws overhand. Fine motor: safety scissors, copies circles, then squares and triangles; draws a 3-part person at ~4, 6 parts at ~5; uses a fork and spoon; dresses with help on buttons/zippers.
Piaget: Preoperational
Symbolic thinking (words and pretend stand for things). Egocentric: sees only their own view; may think they caused something bad (illness = punishment). Magical thinking (thoughts make things happen) and animism (objects are alive). Centration: focuses on one feature. Beginning logic.
Erikson: Initiative vs. Guilt
Loves planning activities, pretending, and trying new things. Confidence grows through success. Too much criticism or control leads to guilt. Support initiative by letting them help with care (hold the tape, choose a bandage). Don't tell them their imaginary friend isn't real.
Language
  • Vocabulary explodes to over 2,000 words by age 5 (textbook: 2,100+); sentences of 4+ words by age 4.
  • Speaks in complete sentences; follows multi-step directions; asks endless "why" and "how" questions.
  • Takes words literally: avoid phrases like "a little stick" or "put you to sleep."
  • Most speech should be understandable to strangers by age 4. Some normal dysfluency (repeating words) can happen; persistent stuttering warrants a speech referral.
Play
Associative play
Plays with other children, shares toys and materials, but with limited organization (no shared goal).
Cooperative play
Develops toward the end of the preschool years: organized group play with shared goals and rules; builds teamwork and problem-solving.

Nursing: encourage imaginative play (dress-up, doctor kits), reading and language-rich activities, and active play; limit recreational screen time (≤1 hr/day until 5).

🏫2.13 Preschool Behavior, Discipline, School Readiness, Safety & MaltreatmentSlides 27–28
Common behaviors (expected)
  • Developing self-control and emotional regulation
  • Active imagination and imaginary friends
  • Curiosity about body differences (normal; answer simply and honestly)
  • Increasing independence; many fears (dark, monsters, being hurt)
Positive discipline
  • Consistent routines and expectations
  • Praise and positive reinforcement
  • Logical, age-appropriate consequences; short time-outs (about 1 min per year of age)
  • Avoid corporal punishment (spanking)
🎒 Preparing for school
Encourage self-care skills (dressing, toileting, hand washing), sharing and cooperation, and emotional resilience and confidence. Continued language growth supports school readiness.
Health promotion & injury prevention
Health promotion
Keep immunizations current (DTaP, IPV, MMR, varicella boosters at 4–6 years); balanced diet and daily activity (hop, skip, jump, tricycle); dental care every 6 months; sleep 10–13 hr/day; hand hygiene and illness prevention.
Injury prevention
Unintentional injury is the leading cause of death. Car seat → forward-facing harness → booster until the seat belt fits (about 4'9", ages 8–12); back seat until 13. Helmet every bike ride (sits above the eyebrows, doesn't slide). Water safety and constant supervision. Firearms stored unloaded and locked, ammunition separate. Prevent poisoning, burns, falls.
Recognizing child maltreatment

Nurses must recognize and report suspected physical, sexual, and emotional abuse, neglect, and factitious disorder imposed on another (FDIA). Reporting starts an investigation; it is not an accusation or automatic removal from the home.

TypeRed flags
Physical abuseBruises in unusual places (chest, abdomen, back, buttocks, ears, neck, genitals; accidental bruises are on bony areas like shins and forehead), bruises in different stages of healing, bite marks, patterned burns (immersion "stocking/glove" burns, cigarette burns), spiral fractures, multiple rib fractures, fractures in different stages of healing, skull fractures, history that doesn't match the injury or the child's development. Unit 2 – Mod 5 – School-Age Children: 6 to 12 Years

Slides: Lifespan Pt 2 (3–10) · Textbook: Unit 2 Mod 5
⚽2.14 School-Age Physical, Cognitive & Psychosocial DevelopmentPt 2 Slides 4–6
Physical
Steady, predictable growth (about 2 in and 4–7 lb per year). Permanent teeth replace baby teeth starting ~6 years. Strength, coordination, and endurance improve. Body image and self-image matter more. Early puberty signs may start in older school-age kids (especially girls).
Motor
Gross: better balance, agility, sports and recreation. Fine: neater handwriting, drawing, musical instruments, tools and technology, independent self-care.
Piaget: Concrete operational
Thinks logically about concrete (real, hands-on) situations. Understands cause and effect; classifies and organizes into categories (loves collections!); develops problem-solving; understands conservation (amount stays the same when shape changes) and reversibility. Still struggles with abstract ideas.
Erikson: Industry vs. Inferiority
Builds competence through learning and achievement; values praise and recognition; self-esteem grows with successes. Repeated failure or criticism → feelings of inferiority. In the hospital, keep up schoolwork and give them tasks they can master.

Language & play: vocabulary and communication expand; reads independently and develops critical thinking. Enjoys cooperative play, organized sports, clubs, and hobbies with rules. Peer group and same-sex friendships become important.

Nursing considerations
Monitor growth on standardized charts; assess milestones and physical activity; encourage at least 60 minutes of daily physical activity; explain the why of procedures and let them take part.
🧩2.15 School-Age Health Promotion, Bullying, Stress & Learning/Behavioral ConcernsPt 2 Slides 7–10
🏃 Activity
≥60 min of moderate-to-vigorous activity daily. Supports heart health, healthy weight, bones, and mood. Sports safety: helmets, mouth guards, and concussion awareness.
🥗 Nutrition
Fruits, veggies, whole grains, lean protein, low-fat dairy. Limit sugary drinks and processed foods. Family meals when possible. Obesity: BMI ≥95th percentile (overweight 85th–<95th).
😴 Sleep
9–12 hours each night. Consistent bedtime routines improve learning, attention, and behavior.

Reinforce healthy habits at every well-child visit, encourage family participation, and start assessing for risk-taking behaviors as children become more independent. Dental sealants; bus, bike, pedestrian, and water safety; supervise unsupervised time (latchkey).

Bullying
Physical, verbal, relational (exclusion, gossip), or cyberbullying. Highest in middle school; higher risk for LGBTQ+ youth. Linked to anxiety, depression, school avoidance, falling grades, sleep problems, self-harm, and psychosomatic complaints.
Stress
Sources: academic expectations, peers, family changes (divorce, moves), extracurricular overload, chronic illness. Shows up as appetite change, new bed-wetting, sleep problems, nightmares, vague stomachaches/headaches.

School nurses manage chronic conditions, give medications, teach health, support mental health, and promote healthy school environments.

Learning disabilities & behavioral/mental health concerns
  • Dyslexia: reading
  • Dysgraphia: writing
  • Dyscalculia: math
  • Nonverbal learning disabilities
  • Anxiety and depression
  • ADHD: inattention, hyperactivity, impulsivity, starting before age 12; treated with medication, behavior therapy, counseling, and school supports (not cured)
  • Behavioral/conduct disorders; trouble with emotional regulation
  • Bowel/bladder concerns: nocturnal enuresis (bed-wetting; more common in boys; primary vs. secondary), encopresis
Nursing considerations
Encourage early developmental and educational evaluations; collaborate with families, schools, and the team; promote an inclusive learning environment; recognize changes in mood, behavior, or grades that need more assessment.
🩺 Clinical application (slide 10)
An 8-year-old visits the school nurse several times a week with headaches and stomachaches. The physical exam is normal with no fever; grades are falling and the child is withdrawn. Think psychosocial cause: assess for bullying/cyberbullying, emotional well-being and anxiety, school performance and learning problems; talk with parents and teachers; refer as needed. Physical complaints in school-age children may be how emotional stress shows up.

Unit 2 – Mod 6 – Adolescents: 12 to 20 Years

Slides: Lifespan Pt 2 (11–17) · Textbook: Unit 2 Mod 6
🌗2.16 Puberty, Adolescent Thinking, Identity & BehaviorsPt 2 Slides 12–13
Puberty
Rapid growth spurt in height and weight (can start ~10; ends when growth plates close); secondary sexual characteristics develop (breast budding is usually the first sign in girls; testicular enlargement in boys); hormones affect body and emotions; acne, body odor, body hair. Individual timing varies a lot. Girls generally start ~2 years earlier than boys. Sexual maturity is staged with Tanner stages.
Piaget: Formal operational
Abstract thinking, logical reasoning, future planning, hypothetical "what if" thinking, ethical and moral reasoning.
Erikson: Identity vs. Role Confusion
Develops a personal identity; explores beliefs and values; increasing independence from parents; peer relationships strongly influence development; romantic relationships develop; explores sexual identity.
Self-esteem & relationships

Self-esteem is shaped by body image, peer acceptance, school achievement, family relationships, and social media. Adolescents need accurate, evidence-based sexual health education and respect for privacy and confidentiality.

⚠️ Risk-taking behaviors
Because the brain (especially the prefrontal "brakes") keeps maturing into the early 20s, adolescents are more likely to engage in reckless driving, substance use, alcohol and drug experimentation, vaping/nicotine, unsafe sex, and risky social media behaviors (sexting). They often feel invincible ("personal fable").
Nursing considerations
Build trust through respectful communication; interview the adolescent alone for part of the visit; explain confidentiality and its limits (risk of harm to self or others, abuse); encourage healthy decision-making; give confidential, developmentally appropriate education.

🧤3.1 Infection Control & Isolation PrecautionsTextbook + slide 6

Young children are at higher infection risk: they put their hands in their mouths, have immature immune systems, and are seen by many providers moving room to room. Hand hygiene is the single most important way to prevent spread, for staff, patients, families, and visitors.

Standard precautions are used for every patient: treat all blood, body fluids (except sweat), broken skin, and mucous membranes as potentially infectious (hand hygiene, PPE as needed, safe injections). Transmission-based precautions are added on top for specific germs.

Donning (putting on) PPE
Hand hygiene → gown → mask/respirator → goggles/face shield → gloves (over the gown cuffs).
Doffing (taking off) PPE
Gloves first (most contaminated) → goggles (from the back) → gown (roll inside out) → mask → hand hygiene. The front of the gown, goggles, and mask are all contaminated.

Private rooms reduce infection spread and support family-centered care (room for a parent to stay). If a parent refuses vaccines, educate, document, and notify the provider.

🧠3.7 Meningitis: Viral & BacterialTextbook

Meningitis is inflammation of the meninges (the protective covering of the brain and spinal cord). It raises intracranial pressure (ICP). You can't tell viral from bacterial by symptoms alone, so every suspected case gets a lumbar puncture and is treated as bacterial until proven otherwise.

🦠 Viral (aseptic) meningitis
Caused by enteroviruses (most common), herpesviruses, adenoviruses. Spreads through mucus, saliva, and stool; more common in summer and fall. Usually milder and self-limiting, but serious in newborns, kids under 5, and the immunocompromised. HSV meningitis is treated with IV acyclovir (monitor kidney function, CBC, I&O; infuse over 1 hr; keep hydrated).
🚨 Bacterial meningitis
Caused by Neisseria meningitidis (meningococcus), Streptococcus pneumoniae, and group B strep (Hib is now rare thanks to the vaccine). Spreads through saliva, nasal discharge, sharing drinks/utensils; can follow sinusitis or ear infections. Risk: close contact, dorms/communal living, under-vaccination. More severe: can rapidly cause sepsis, hearing loss, hydrocephalus, or death.

Kernig & Brudzinski signs
Kernig: pain/resistance when straightening the knee with the hip flexed. Brudzinski: flexing the neck makes the hips and knees flex. Both signal meningeal irritation, but a negative sign does NOT rule out meningitis, especially in infants.
🔴 Petechial/purpuric rash
A non-blanching petechial rash points to meningococcal infection (meningococcemia). It is an emergency: untreated, the tissue under the rash can become necrotic (gangrene).

Nursing care
  • Droplet precautions as soon as meningitis is suspected, until 24 hours of IV antibiotics. Viral meningitis in a diapered/incontinent child: add contact precautions.
  • CSF culture takes up to 48 hr, so start empiric IV antibiotics immediately after cultures are collected. Delay increases death and disability. Corticosteroids may reduce hearing loss in bacterial meningitis.
  • Close contacts get prophylactic antibiotics (within 3 ft for more than 8 hr in the 7 days before, through 24 hr after, the start of antibiotics).
  • Monitor for increased ICP: level of consciousness, pupils, vital signs, fontanel in infants, seizures (report them).
  • Monitor I&O and hydration; report hyponatremia (possible SIADH).
  • Comfort: dark, quiet room (photophobia, headache), head of bed ≥30°, neutral body alignment, weight-based antipyretics/analgesics.
  • Prevention: Hib, pneumococcal, and meningococcal vaccines; hand hygiene; treat ear/throat infections early.

😟3.9 The Stress of Hospitalization: How Children & Families RespondTextbook

Children are scared of unfamiliar places, people, IVs, needles, and equipment. The three big stressors are separation, loss of control, and fear of pain/injury. The child's developmental stage shapes the response.

🤝3.10 Nursing Interventions: Communication, Safety, Consent, Procedures & RestraintsTextbook
🗣️ AIDET communication
Acknowledge (welcome the child and family) · Introduce (name and role) · Duration (how long things will take) · Explanation (plan, procedures) · Thank you (and invite feedback).
🤞 Establishing trust: never…
…call medicine "candy," make up an answer you don't know (say you'll find out), promise "everything will be okay," or say a procedure won't hurt. Use honest, age-appropriate words: "This will feel like a quick pinch." Keep professional boundaries (no social media connections).
Admission & reducing stress
  • Orient the child and family to the room, call light, playroom, and rules; share client and family rights (be told about care, help make decisions, voice opinions and complaints).
  • Include the family in the plan; maintain home routines (sleep, meals, rituals) as much as possible.
  • Use the treatment/procedure room for painful procedures (IV starts) so the bed stays a safe space. The playroom is a "no procedures" zone.
  • Play: lullabies, mobiles, and mirrors for infants; blocks, crayons, and balls for toddlers; medical play and puppets for preschoolers; games, crafts, and video calls for school-age kids and teens. Pounding toys help release anger. Bring activities to the room if the child is isolated.
  • Teach-back: have the family explain instructions in their own words; check understanding throughout (chunk and check), not just at the end. Repeating word-for-word doesn't prove understanding. Start discharge teaching early, not at the last minute.
Safety
Environment
Two identifiers; ID bands; crib rails up whenever the nurse steps away; age-appropriate bed (open crib for infants; enclosed "cage" crib for toddlers who climb); safe sleep on the back for infants in the hospital too (no co-sleeping); sharps out of reach; fall prevention; standardized handoff (IPASS or SBAR).
Latex allergy
Higher risk with spina bifida or many surgeries. Reactions range from rash to wheezing to anaphylaxis. Cross-reactive foods: banana, avocado, kiwi, chestnut. Use latex-free supplies (gloves, catheters, tourniquets, BP cuffs, tape, balloons, nipples, pacifiers).

IV sites: assess every hour with the touch, look, compare method (touch the site, look at the insertion site, compare with the opposite limb) to catch infiltration and phlebitis early.

Informed consent, minors & assent
  • Before signing, parents must understand the diagnosis, the procedure and why it's recommended, risks and benefits, alternatives (with their risks/benefits), and the risks of doing nothing. Use interpreters and plain language. Obtaining consent is the provider's job; the nurse witnesses, advocates, and alerts the provider if the family still has questions.
  • Minors (under 18): consent comes from the parent or legal guardian; confirm who has custody.
  • Emancipated minors consent for themselves: married, in the military, or legally separated from parents.
  • Implied consent: emergencies, for life-saving care.
  • Assent: the child's agreement. Not legally required, but it shows respect; ask for assent when you will honor a "no."
Preparing for procedures & surgery
  • Be honest; use words the child understands; show equipment; let them touch it; describe how it will feel (cold, pressure, texture).
  • Under 5: prepare a day or two before (or right before for toddlers). Older children: several days to a week ahead. Kids over 4 do well with simple information.
  • Know the prep: NPO, full bladder, or none. Labs: blood (venipuncture, heel or finger stick, or from a line per protocol), urine (clean catch, or catheter if sterile), swabs.
  • Pre-op tours and child life specialists reduce surgical anxiety.
  • For infants: skin-to-skin contact and oral sucrose; for all: distraction (bubbles, games), parent present for comfort.
Restraints
Rules
A last resort after alternatives (distraction, child life, family, comfort items). Need a provider order (except emergencies) stating the type, reason, duration, and behaviors. Use the least restrictive option and remove ASAP. Assistive personnel can help apply them, but only a nurse monitors a restrained child: vital signs, skin, circulation, ROM, IV site, and dignity. Don't say "swaddle restraint" to parents (it upsets them); explain the purpose.
Types
Swaddle (mummy) restraint: younger infants for brief exams/procedures. Immobilization board: older infants and toddlers. Elbow restraints: keep hands from pulling tubes/drains or touching a cleft lip repair. Using all four crib side rails counts as a restraint.
💊3.11 Medication Administration, Injections, Procedures & Daily CareTextbook
Dosage safety
  • Pediatric doses are weight-based (mg/kg). Convert first: 1 kg = 2.2 lb; 1 lb = 16 oz. Always check that the ordered dose is within the safe dose range before giving it. Round only at the end, per facility policy.
  • Medication reconciliation on admission, transfer, and discharge (include vitamins and herbal supplements).
  • Routes: PO, SL, PR, IV, IM, subcut, eye, ear, inhaled, topical/transdermal. Feeding tubes (NG, G-tube) are used when the child can't eat or swallow meds.
🥄 Giving oral liquid medications
  • Use a calibrated oral syringe, dropper, or medicine cup. Never household spoons.
  • Infants: semi-upright; gently pull down the chin; place the syringe in the side of the cheek (buccal pocket) and give slowly in small amounts; let them suck on a nipple/pacifier between doses; softly blowing on the face triggers swallowing.
  • Don't mix meds into a full bottle of formula/breast milk or a big serving of food (they may not finish it, and it can cause food aversion). A small amount of water or applesauce is okay.
  • No honey for children under 12 months (botulism).
  • Count drops onto a spoon if ordered in drops. Offer a choice of drink afterward for older kids. Give in an upright position to prevent aspiration.
Injections & procedures (skills)

Medication administration can't be delegated to assistive personnel (an RN may delegate to another RN or LPN who is competent). Always follow the rights of medication administration (right child, medication, dose, route, time, documentation, plus assessment, evaluation, refusal, education), use two identifiers, check allergies, and prepare meds for one child at a time.

💡 Test tip: aspiration
Do not aspirate when giving vaccines or subcutaneous injections. The recommended sites have no large blood vessels, and skipping it makes the injection faster and less painful.
Nutrition, diets, sleep & fever
Calorie needs
Infants about 100 kcal/kg/day; 1–3 yr 80; 4–5 yr 70; 6–8 yr 65; 9+ yr 35–45 kcal/kg/day. Picky, sick kids eat better with others and when they help choose foods. Don't force or use strong-smelling foods.
Liquid diets
Clear liquids (see-through): water, broth, clear juice without pulp, popsicles (no fruit pieces), gelatin, sports drinks, hard candy. Full liquids = clears plus milk, pudding, ice cream, strained cream soups, juice with pulp, milkshakes, supplements.
Sleep needs (24 hr)
Newborns 14–17 · infants 12–16 (with naps) · toddlers 11–14 · preschoolers 10–13 · school-age 9–12 · adolescents 8–10.
Fever
≥ 38°C (100.4°F). If the child is still eating, drinking, playing, and voiding, it can run its course; antipyretics for discomfort. Seek care for underlying illness, rash, dehydration, or persistence. Can also follow teething, overdressing, or immunizations.

Hygiene & elimination: keep bathing and tooth-brushing routines; expect temporary toilet-training regression in toddlers. Ostomies: assess the stoma and skin and empty/change the pouch (consult the wound/ostomy team). Activity: keep kids moving as able.

🔑 Big picture
Prioritize by continuous assessment (ABCs first), keep the family at the center, use honest, developmentally appropriate communication, and protect the child from both physical harm (dosing errors, falls, latex, infection) and emotional harm (separation, loss of control, fear).

🚶A.2 Toddlers: Nutrition Screening, Sun Safety & Dental HabitsUnit 2 Mod 3
Malnutrition cutoffs
Possible undernutrition: below the 15th percentile, or dropping across two or more major percentile lines. Possible overnutrition: above the 85th percentile. Not every child outside these lines is malnourished (genetics matter); assess further, including hair loss, dry skin, and fatigue.
Hunger Vital Sign (food insecurity screen)
The AAP recommends screening all families. Two statements, answered "often true," "sometimes true," or "never true": (1) "In the past 12 months, we worried our food would run out before we got money to buy more." (2) "The food we bought just didn't last, and we didn't have money to get more." "Often" or "sometimes" to either = positive → connect to food resources, nonjudgmentally.
☀️ Sunburn prevention
Avoid direct sun 10 a.m.–4 p.m.; UV rays get through on cloudy days too. Sunscreen SPF 15 or higher, applied 30 minutes before going out and reapplied about every 2 hours; SPF clothing and a wide-brim hat. Sunburn shows up 6–12 hours after exposure: cool compresses, aloe, extra fluids, acetaminophen or ibuprofen.
🦷 Dental habits
Avoid sippy cups for long periods (fluid pools around the teeth). Replace toothbrushes every 3–4 months or after illness. Thumb-sucking and pacifiers usually stop between 2 and 4 years; longer use can misalign teeth.
🎨A.3 Preschoolers: Nutrition, Activity, Speech, Aggression & SafetyUnit 2 Mod 4
Nutrition & sleep promotion
1,000–1,800 kcal/day of nutrient-dense food; keep offering a variety (picky eating continues). Healthy eating can be affordable with planning, seasonal produce, and frozen or canned options. Sleep: consistent routine, no screens before bed (and none in the bedroom), cool and quiet room, comfort object, reassurance for fears.
Physical activity & media
At least 180 minutes/day, including 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity (tag, playground, tricycle, swimming). Bone strengthening: hopping, skipping, jumping rope. A lack of safe places to play is a common barrier. Media: high-quality programs, co-viewed with a parent.
Speech issues

About 1 in 12 U.S. children ages 3–17 has a communication disorder. Stuttering is linked to emotional reactivity, poor self-regulation, and anxiety in social situations (a new classroom, hospitalization). If not addressed, it can lead to social anxiety, teasing, or behavior problems. Early intervention works best; fun activities (songs, rhymes, riddles, storytelling) help speech develop.

Aggression

Aggression is an expected reaction when a child can't yet express anger in acceptable ways. It peaks around age 3 and should decline significantly by age 5 as children learn to see other perspectives. Concerning signs: repeated patterns and clear intent to harm. Helping children recognize emotions reduces aggression.

Safety extras
🤸 Trampolines
Discouraged for children 6 and under (fractures of arms, legs, head, neck). If used: ground level on soft, even ground, away from trees/fences; padded frame and springs; safety net; inspect before each use; always supervise; one jumper at a time; no flips.
🏊 Water & poisoning
Drowning is the most common cause of unintentional injury death under age 4. It's silent and fast. Never leave a young child in the care of another child; fence pools with a self-closing, self-latching gate; parents learn CPR. Poisoning: mostly from unsecured chemicals and medicines; childproof caps (Poison Prevention Packaging Act of 1970); post the Poison Control number.
Sexuality, body safety & abuse
  • Childhood masturbation (ages 2–5) is common and developmentally expected; redirect, don't punish.
  • Sexual abuse facts: about 90% is by someone the child or family knows and trusts; about 1 in 4 girls and 1 in 20 boys experience it. Effects can be lifelong: STIs (including HPV-related cancers), depression, anxiety, PTSD, suicide risk, substance use, risky sexual behavior. An STI may not show on the first test, so retest if symptoms appear.
  • Verbal/emotional abuse (yelling, name-calling, rejection, threats) is the most common form of family violence. Signs: behavior changes, speech problems, developmental delays, withdrawal, fear of doing wrong, extreme passivity or aggression.

About 18.5% of U.S. children are obese. Boys are more often obese at 6–11 years; girls during adolescence. Obese children are more likely to stay obese as adults. Check BMI at least yearly.

💩 Encopresis
Repeated (usually involuntary) passing of stool into clothing after age 4. Usually caused by long-standing constipation: liquid stool leaks around a hard mass (can look like diarrhea). More common in boys. Risks: withholding, low fiber, too much dairy, low fluids, constipating meds, ADHD, ASD, anxiety, stress. Treatment: high-fiber diet, plenty of water, regular toileting routine, patience and positive reinforcement (no shaming).
😰 PTSD
Diagnosed when trauma symptoms last more than 1 month or interfere with life: reliving the event, nightmares, sleep problems, numbness, irritability, being easily startled, avoiding reminders, denial. Can be mistaken for ADHD. Treatment: psychotherapy, behavior therapy, medication; help the child feel safe.
Stress & fears
Sudden stress raises heart rate, breathing, and BP and causes GI upset and headaches. Physical signs: appetite change, new bed-wetting, nightmares, vague stomachaches. Behavior signs: anxiety, clinging, anger, regression, withdrawing from activities. Mild fears (dark, being alone, big animals) are common. Small setbacks build resilience.
Self-esteem & discipline
Self-esteem is tied to school success and parental trust and support; it improves adaptability and relationships. Corporal punishment is linked to impaired emotional development and more aggression; it doesn't teach positive behavior.
Substances, sexuality & online life
  • Substance use can start at 9–10 years (alcohol, tobacco, marijuana, others' prescriptions). About 21.6 million children live with a parent who misuses substances. Risk: mental health problems, parents modeling use, disengaged parents, feeling unsafe. Protective: self-regulation, reliable caregivers, safety, good friendships. Screen at well visits.
  • Sexuality education should respect personal, cultural, and religious values and be built into routine, nonjudgmental health teaching.
  • Internet safety: keep personal info private (name, school, photos, passwords); learn to spot unreliable sources and suspicious links; parents should tell kids they'll monitor online activity (builds trust) and focus on quality, not just time. 2023 Surgeon General advisory on social media and mental health.
🔫 Firearms
Leading cause of injury death under 18. Safest home has no guns. Otherwise: unloaded, locked, ammunition stored separately, kids can't reach keys or combinations. Over one-third of unintentional child shootings happen at friends' or relatives' homes, so ask before playdates.
🚌 Bus safety
Wait at least 10 feet from the curb; board only when the driver signals; stay seated; never walk behind the bus or stop near it to pick something up; make eye contact with the driver before crossing in front.
🎧A.5 Adolescents: Puberty, Reproductive Health, Pregnancy, Mental Health & RiskUnit 2 Mod 6
Puberty changes
Females: breast development, pubic and axillary hair, menstruation, hands/feet grow first. Males: testicular then penile enlargement, pubic/axillary/facial hair, voice changes, broader shoulders and muscle, erections and ejaculation. Growth spurt 4–6 inches/year. Girls stop growing about 2–2.5 years after menarche (menarche ~12–14); boys grow until 18–20. Poor nutrition can delay puberty and stunt growth.

Treatment: SSRIs, OTC pain relievers, birth control pills, exercise, stress reduction.

🤰 Adolescent pregnancy
Higher risk of preterm birth, low birth weight, and high blood pressure, plus stigma, isolation, and money problems. 💊 Emergency contraception (levonorgestrel)
Take within 72 hours (some effect up to 5 days). Delays ovulation; not used if already pregnant. Not a regular birth control method, no STI protection, use a barrier method or abstinence for 7 days afterward. Side effects: irregular bleeding, nausea, headache.
Mental health & risk behaviors
  • Eating disorder warning signs: rapid weight change, preoccupation with weight and saying "I'm fat," extreme dieting, skipping meals or avoiding eating with others, hiding food.   • Substance use warning signs: behavior and mood changes, changes in sleep and eating, falling grades. Treatment steps: assessment → detoxification → rehabilitation and counseling → aftercare (as long as needed to prevent relapse).
  • Suicide and self-harm: suicide is a leading cause of adolescent death. Risk factors: mental illness, substance use, family conflict or trauma, bullying, social isolation, academic or peer pressure. Self-harm (cutting, burning) signals underlying distress. Refer to mental health and teach parents the warning signs.
  • Why teens take risks: peer pressure, little parental involvement, mental health problems, impulsivity, lack of accurate information. About 10,000 adolescents died from preventable risk-related causes from 2018 to 2023.
  • Injury prevention: seat belts every ride, no texting while driving, helmets, safe sex, plus sleep, nutrition, and activity teaching.
  • Hypertension (≥130/80): lifestyle changes first; lisinopril (ACE inhibitor) if needed. Watch for cough, dizziness, and high potassium; avoid salt substitutes.
